Through Different Eyes

Experience accessibility barriers firsthand

Welcome to the Simulation

In the next few minutes, you'll experience what it's like to be a student encountering accessibility barriers in your course materials.

Each scenario shows you two perspectives: what your instructor intended to create, and what a student with a disability actually experiences.

Pay attention to your frustration level. This is what your students feel every day.

How This Works

You'll encounter 4 scenarios:

👁️ The Unreadable Syllabus — Experience low vision and blindness

🦻 The Silent Lecture — Experience deafness and hard of hearing

🧠 The Cognitive Overload — Experience dyslexia and cognitive disabilities

🖐️ The Impossible Quiz — Experience motor and mobility challenges

In each scenario: Make choices and see how barriers compound. Your frustration meter tracks the emotional toll. At the end, you'll get actionable fixes you can implement in your own courses.

0%
How this one works: Read the left panel first — it's the plain-language overview your professor posted. Then move to the reading on the right and see how long it takes to find those same concepts and questions buried inside it.
✓ What the Instructor Intended

Reading Assignment: Chapter 3

Main Idea: Digital media has transformed how we consume information.

Key Points:

  • Speed of information distribution
  • Democratization of content creation
  • New forms of misinformation

Questions to Consider:

  • How has social media changed journalism?
  • What are the benefits and drawbacks?

Keep these concepts and questions in mind as you complete the following reading.

✗ What the Student Actually Experiences

Reading

It has been observed by researchers that the transformation of media consumption patterns, which has been facilitated by digital technologies, has resulted in significant alterations to the mechanisms through which information is disseminated, consumed, and interpreted by various stakeholder groups.

Furthermore, it should be noted that the democratization of content creation — a phenomenon that has been widely theorized within the scholarly literature — has engendered a corresponding proliferation of epistemic fragmentation, whereby the capacity of institutional gatekeepers to adjudicate the veracity of disseminated claims has been substantially attenuated.

Consequently, it is incumbent upon stakeholders operating within this transformed informational ecosystem to cultivate the requisite media literacy competencies necessary to navigate the attendant epistemological challenges that have arisen as a function of these structural shifts.

There it is — highlighted above. It's the exact same idea as the left panel, just wrapped in clauses, passive voice, and jargon that make it hard to spot. Most readers have to decode the sentence two or three times before they can confirm it says what the instructor's overview already told them.

"I've read this paragraph five times. I know every individual word, but I can't hold the sentence in my head long enough to find the actual point. By the time I reach the period, I've forgotten how it started."

Reflection & Action

What You Just Experienced

In just a few minutes, you encountered barriers that your students face every single day. You felt the frustration, the time pressure, the sense of being excluded from the learning experience. Now imagine feeling this way in every class, every semester.

The Good News

Most of these barriers are easy to fix. You don't need special software or expensive tools. You just need to think about accessibility from the start—when you design your syllabus, record your lectures, structure your readings, and build your assessments.

Reflect on Your Experience

Which scenario frustrated you the most? That's where your students need the most help. What would have made it easier? That's your action item.
