Interactive Faculty Development Activity

Through Different Eyes

Experience accessibility barriers firsthand

In the next few minutes, you'll experience what it's like to be a student encountering accessibility barriers when engaging with course activites and materials.

Each scenario shows you two perspectives: what the instructor intended to create, and what a student with a disability actually experiences.

Pay attention to your frustration level. This is what some students feel every day.

Orientation

Before You Begin

You'll move through four scenarios, each from the perspective of a student encountering a different kind of barrier. On the left, you'll see what the instructor intended. On the right, you'll see what the student actually experiences.

At each decision point, choose how the student responds. There's no "win" — only different costs. A frustration meter will track the emotional and cognitive toll as barriers compound across the simulation.

At the end of each scenario, you'll get a concrete UDL Tip — a specific, implementable fix tied to a Universal Design for Learning principle.

Scenario 1 of 4 · Low Vision / Blindness

👁️ The Unreadable Syllabus

It's the first day. Your professor posted the syllabus to the course site. You open it using your screen reader.

What the Instructor Intended

Syllabus.pdf — Page 1

Course Overview

This course explores the history of digital media and its impact on society. Students will analyze primary sources, engage in critical discussions, and produce original research.

Textbook


Photo by Nenad Kaevik on Unsplash

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, you will be able to:

  • Analyze media critically
  • Conduct original research
  • Present findings effectively

Grading

Participation: 20% | Assignments: 40% | Final Project: 40%

What the Student Experiences

Course Overview

This course explores the history of digital media and its impact on society. Students will analyze primary sources, engage in critical discussions, and produce original research.

Textbook


Photo by Nenad Kaevik on Unsplash

🔊 "Image. Image. Image. Page 1 of 4."

The screen reader cannot extract any text because the entire document has no tags and is a scanned image. There is no way to know what the grading breakdown says, when office hours are, or what the textbook is, without seeing the picture.

What does the student do?

Student Frustration Level 0%

REPRESENTATION UDL TIP

Export documents as real text, not scanned images — a quick way to check is trying to highlight text in the PDF. Add alt text to all images and charts. Use built-in heading styles (not just bold/bigger font) so screen readers can navigate the structure.

Scenario 2 of 4 · Deaf / Hard of Hearing

🦻 The Silent Lecture

Today's lecture was recorded and posted online. You missed class and need to catch up before the quiz.

What the Instructor Intended

[0:00] "The printing press, invented by Gutenberg around 1440, revolutionized information distribution across Europe."
[0:16] "This democratized knowledge and fundamentally challenged existing ecclesiastical and political power structures."
[0:33] "For your midterm, remember these three key dates: 1440, 1517, and 1536."
[0:52] "Today we'll explore how digital media is having a strikingly similar disruptive effect on modern institutions."

What the Student Experiences

[0:00] "the printing press invented by good enberg around fourteen-forty revolutionized information distribution across Europe"
[0:17] "this democracitized knowledge and fundamentally challenged existing uh political power structures"
[0:33] buffering
The exam dates were never captioned. By the time the system caught up, the professor had already moved to the next slide.

What does the student do?

Student Frustration Level 0%

REPRESENTATION UDL TIP

Auto-captions are a starting point, not a finish line — budget time to review and correct them, especially for names, dates, and technical terms. Post critical info (exam dates, assignment details) in writing on the syllabus or LMS, never spoken-only.

Scenario 3 of 4 · Dyslexia / Cognitive

🧠 The Cognitive Overload

What the Instructor Intended

Read Chapter 7, pages 120-138. Come to class prepared to discuss the content.

What the Student Experiences

Notwithstanding the aforementioned epistemological framework, which — as previously theorized by scholars operating within the broader post-structuralist tradition — necessarily complicates any attempt to reconcile the hegemonic discourse with its ostensibly counter-hegemonic alternatives...

No headings. No summary. No defined terms. Eighteen more pages exactly like this.

What does the student do?

Student Frustration Level 0%

REPRESENTATION UDL TIP

Provide a reading guide with the reading assignment that outlines key take-aways and a glossary of new terms. When assigning a full chapter, point students to the specific pages or sections that matter most rather than leaving them to guess.

Scenario 4 of 4 · Motor / Mobility

🖐️ The Impossible Quiz

A timed quiz just opened. You have limited fine motor control and use a switch device instead of a mouse.
⏱ Time Remaining 00:45

What the Instructor Intended

A quick, engaging drag-and-drop matching quiz — match each term to its definition by dragging small cards into place, timed to keep students focused and prevent looking things up.

What the Student Experiences

Tiny draggable targets, placed close together, with no keyboard alternative:

AB CD

Each drag attempt requires precise, sustained cursor control. Missed drags don't just fail — they cost time the strict timer won't give back.

What does the student do?

Student Frustration Level 0%

ACTION & EXPRESSION UDL TIP

Offer a non-drag-and-drop alternative for the same question (e.g., dropdowns or multiple choice), and build in generous target sizes, spacing, and flexible timing. Most institutional LMS platforms are WCAG 2.1 AA compliant — using your LMS's native quiz/exam tools (rather than embedded third-party widgets or PDFs) will often give students an accessible format automatically.

Debrief & Reflection

One Thing You Probably Noticed — But We Didn't Label

Every UDL Tip in this simulation pointed to Representation or Action & Expression. That wasn't an accident, but it wasn't the whole story either.

The frustration meter climbing with each scenario? That's Engagement — and honestly, it's the principle this whole activity is actually about. Every time a student had to decide whether to push through alone, send that email, or just give up, they were spending energy that should've gone toward learning — not surviving the format.

Fixing the document is the easy part. Reducing the hesitation before a student hits "send" on an accommodation request — that's harder, and it starts before you ever open your syllabus template. (CAST UDL Guidelines)

Reflection Prompts

Which scenario felt most familiar to a barrier you may have unintentionally created in your own course?
Did you notice yourself choosing the "advocate" option less often as frustration increased? What does that suggest about the real cost of self-advocacy?
What's one thing you can fix in your course this week — not next semester?
